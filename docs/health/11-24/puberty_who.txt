Understanding Puberty and Menstruation: A Guide for Young Women (Ages 11–24)

Puberty is a natural biological process that marks the transition from childhood to adulthood. For girls, it typically begins between ages 8 and 13, with the average onset around age 11 in India. During puberty, the body undergoes several changes driven by hormones, primarily estrogen and progesterone.

Key Physical Changes During Puberty:
- Breast development (thelarche) is usually the first visible sign, beginning around ages 8–13.
- Growth of pubic and underarm hair.
- A growth spurt in height, typically peaking around age 12.
- Widening of the hips and changes in body composition.
- Onset of menstruation (menarche), which usually occurs 2–3 years after breast development begins.

Understanding Menstruation:
Menstruation is the monthly shedding of the uterine lining when pregnancy does not occur. The average menstrual cycle lasts 28 days, though cycles of 21–35 days are considered normal. A period typically lasts 3–7 days.

The menstrual cycle has four phases:
1. Menstrual Phase (Days 1–5): The uterine lining sheds, causing bleeding.
2. Follicular Phase (Days 1–13): The pituitary gland releases FSH, stimulating egg development in the ovaries.
3. Ovulation (Day 14): A mature egg is released from the ovary.
4. Luteal Phase (Days 15–28): The uterus prepares for potential pregnancy; if no implantation occurs, hormone levels drop and the cycle restarts.

Common Menstrual Concerns for Young Women:
- Irregular periods are normal in the first 2–3 years after menarche.
- Dysmenorrhea (period cramps) affects up to 90% of adolescent girls. Mild pain can be managed with heat application, gentle exercise, and over-the-counter pain relief.
- Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS) may include mood changes, bloating, breast tenderness, and fatigue.
- Heavy bleeding (menorrhagia) lasting more than 7 days or requiring pad changes every 1–2 hours warrants medical attention.

Menstrual Hygiene Management:
The WHO and UNICEF recommend access to clean menstrual hygiene products, private washing facilities, and safe disposal methods. Options include:
- Sanitary pads (most commonly used in India)
- Menstrual cups (reusable, eco-friendly, cost-effective)
- Tampons
- Cloth pads (reusable, must be washed and sun-dried properly)

India's Menstrual Hygiene Scheme (MHS), launched in 2011, provides subsidised sanitary napkins to adolescent girls in rural areas through ASHA workers. The scheme covers girls aged 10–19 and distributes packs of 6 pads for ₹6.

When to See a Doctor:
- No periods by age 15 (primary amenorrhea).
- Periods suddenly stop for more than 3 months (secondary amenorrhea).
- Severe pain that interferes with daily activities.
- Extremely heavy or prolonged bleeding.
- Signs of infection such as unusual discharge or odor.

Nutritional Needs During Puberty:
Adolescent girls need increased iron intake (15–18 mg/day) to compensate for menstrual blood loss. The Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) recommends a diet rich in green leafy vegetables, jaggery, dates, and fortified cereals. Calcium (800–1000 mg/day) is critical for bone development during the growth spurt.

The Government of India's Weekly Iron and Folic Acid Supplementation (WIFS) programme provides free iron-folic acid tablets to adolescent girls through schools and Anganwadi centres.

[Source: WHO Adolescent Health Guidelines 2024, UNICEF Menstrual Hygiene Management Guidance, ICMR Dietary Guidelines for Indians 2020, Ministry of Health and Family Welfare — Rashtriya Kishor Swasthya Karyakram (RKSK)]
